# Struggle for Existence (Charles Darwin, On the Origin of Species, chapter III, opening)

Source: Project Gutenberg ebook number 1228, public domain. This text is from 1859 and follows the sixth edition published in 1872. It is used as the declared human source for the machinize demo (paper §11).

The concept of natural selection in a broad context. The exponential growth potential of species. The swift population growth of introduced species and plants. Factors limiting population growth. Competition is widespread. Climate impacts on species. Protection due to population density. Interconnectedness of all living organisms. The fiercest competition occurs among individuals and varieties of the same species; competition is also intense among species within the same genus. The relationship between organisms is the most significant factor in nature.

Before delving into this chapter, I need to make a few introductory comments to explain how the struggle for survival influences natural selection. In the previous chapter, it was demonstrated that among organisms in their natural habitat, there is some individual variability; indeed, I am not aware of any dispute regarding this fact. It is irrelevant to our discussion whether a multitude of uncertain forms should be classified as species, sub-species, or varieties; for example, the question of whether the two or three hundred uncertain forms of British plants should be considered species is less important if we admit the existence of well-defined varieties. However, the mere presence of individual variability and a few well-marked varieties, though essential as the foundation for our work, provides us with only a small part of the understanding needed to explain how species arise in nature. How have all these exquisite adaptations of one part of an organism to another part, and to the conditions of life, and of one distinct organism to another, been refined? We see these beautiful adaptations most clearly in the woodpecker and mistletoe; and only slightly less clearly in the humblest parasite that clings to the hair of a quadruped or the feathers of a bird; in the structure of the beetle that dives through the water; in the feathered seed that is carried by the gentlest breeze; in short, we see beautiful adaptations everywhere and in every part of the organic world.

It might be asked again, how do varieties, which I refer to as incipient species, become fully developed into distinct species that often differ from each other more than do the varieties within a single species? How do groups of species that form what are known as distinct genera, and which differ even more from each other than species within the same genus do, come about? As we will see more fully in the next chapter, these outcomes result inevitably from the struggle for survival. Due to this struggle, any variation, no matter how small and regardless of its cause, if it benefits an individual in its complex interactions with other organisms and the environment, will tend to be preserved and will usually be passed on to its offspring. Consequently, the offspring will have a better chance of survival, as only a small number of the many individuals born at any given time can survive. I have termed this principle, whereby each slight variation that is advantageous is preserved, as Natural Selection, to highlight its connection to human selection. We have observed that through selection, humans can indeed achieve significant results and adapt organisms to their needs, thanks to the accumulation of minor but useful variations provided by nature. However, Natural Selection, as we will later see, is a force that is always ready to act and is vastly superior to human efforts, just as the creations of nature are superior to those of art.

We will now delve into the struggle for existence in greater detail. In my upcoming work, this topic will be explored in more depth. The elder De Candolle and Lyell have extensively and philosophically demonstrated that all living organisms face intense competition. Regarding plants, W. Herbert, Dean of Manchester, has treated this subject with great vigor and expertise, clearly a result of his extensive knowledge in horticulture. It is easy to acknowledge in words the truth of the universal struggle for survival, but it is more challenging to consistently keep this conclusion in mind. Unless it is firmly embedded in one’s thoughts, I am convinced that the entire natural economy, along with every fact concerning distribution, rarity, abundance, extinction, and variation, will be seen only vaguely or misunderstood. We see nature’s face bright with joy, often observing an abundance of food; yet we do not see, or we forget, that the birds singing around us mostly feed on insects or seeds, thus constantly destroying life; we also overlook how extensively these birds, their eggs, or their young are preyed upon by other birds and beasts. We do not always remember that even when food is plentiful now, it is not so throughout the seasons of each year.

I should clarify that I employ the term "Struggle for Existence" in a broad and metaphorical way, encompassing the reliance of one organism on another and, more importantly, not just the survival of the individual but also its success in producing offspring. During a period of scarcity, two dogs might truly be said to struggle with each other to obtain food and stay alive. However, a plant growing on the edge of a desert is said to struggle for survival against the drought, though it is more accurate to say it relies on moisture. A plant that generates a thousand seeds annually, with only one typically reaching maturity, is more accurately described as struggling with other plants of the same and different kinds that already occupy the land. The mistletoe depends on the apple tree and a few other species, but it can only be said to struggle with these trees in a stretched sense, because if too many mistletoes grow on the same tree, it will weaken and eventually die. However, several young mistletoes growing closely together on the same branch can more accurately be described as competing with each other. Since the mistletoe is spread by birds, its survival hinges on birds; it can metaphorically be said to compete with other fruit-producing plants to entice birds to eat and thus disperse its seeds rather than those of other plants. In these various senses, which blend into one another, I use the general term "struggle for existence" for convenience.

The high rate at which all living beings tend to reproduce inevitably leads to a struggle for existence. Any organism that produces several eggs or seeds during its lifetime must face destruction at some point in its life, and during some season or occasional year, otherwise, based on the principle of exponential growth, its numbers would quickly become so large that no habitat could sustain them. Therefore, since more individuals are produced than can survive, there must always be a struggle for existence, whether it is between individuals of the same species, between individuals of different species, or against the environmental conditions. This is the principle of Malthus applied with great force to all animal and plant kingdoms, as there can be no artificial increase in food supply and no prudential control over reproduction. Although some species may currently be increasing in numbers, not all can do so indefinitely, as the world has limited capacity to support them.

Every organic being naturally grows at such a rapid pace that, without destruction, the earth would soon be overwhelmed by the offspring of a single pair. Even humans, who reproduce more slowly, double in number every twenty-five years, and at this rate, within a few thousand years, there would literally be no standing room for their descendants. Linnæus calculated that if an annual plant produced only two seeds—no plant is as unproductive as this—and these seedlings produced two seeds the next year, and so on, there would be a million plants after twenty years. The elephant is considered the slowest breeder among all known animals, and I have carefully estimated its probable minimum rate of natural increase: assuming it breeds when thirty years old and continues to do so until ninety years old, producing three pairs of offspring during this period, it would be an understatement to say that fifteen million elephants would be alive by the end of the fifth century, all descendants of the first pair.

We have better evidence on this subject than mere theoretical calculations, such as the numerous recorded instances of various animals increasing astonishingly quickly in the wild when conditions are favorable for two or three consecutive seasons. Even more striking is the evidence from domestic animals that have gone wild in several parts of the world: if the reported rates of increase of slow-breeding cattle and horses in South America and, more recently, in Australia had not been well substantiated, they would have seemed unbelievable. The same is true for plants: cases can be cited of introduced plants that have become widespread across entire islands within less than ten years. Many of the plants now most abundant over the vast plains of La Plata, covering square leagues of land almost exclusively, were imported from Europe. There are plants that now range from Cape Comorin to the Himalayas in India, which were brought in from America since its discovery. In these and countless other instances, no one thinks that the fertility of these animals or plants has increased suddenly and significantly. The explanation is that the conditions of life have been very favorable, resulting in less destruction of old and young individuals, and nearly all the young surviving to breed. In such cases, the geometrical ratio of increase, which always surprises, simply explains the extraordinarily rapid increase and wide distribution of naturalized species in their new environments.

In nature, nearly every plant generates seeds, and most animals pair annually. Therefore, it is reasonable to claim that all plants and animals are inclined to grow at a geometric rate. They would quickly populate every location where they could survive if not for some form of destruction at some point in their lives. Our acquaintance with larger domesticated animals can be misleading: we observe little destruction among them and overlook the fact that thousands are killed each year for food. In the wild, an equivalent number would need to be eliminated somehow.
