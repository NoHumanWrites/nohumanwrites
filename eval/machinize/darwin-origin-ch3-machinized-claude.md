How the struggle relates to natural selection. The phrase is meant broadly. Populations grow by geometrical powers of increase. Naturalised animals and plants multiply quickly. What kinds of checks hold numbers down. Competition reaches everywhere. Climate has its effects. Large numbers of individuals give protection. Every animal and plant is bound to others by complex relations across the whole of nature. The struggle for life is fiercest between individuals and varieties of one species, and often fierce between species belonging to the same genus. Of all relations, the one between organism and organism matters most.

Before taking up the subject of this chapter I need to set out a few opening remarks, so that it is clear how the struggle for existence bears on Natural Selection. The previous chapter established that organic beings living in a state of nature show some individual variability, and to my knowledge nobody has ever disputed this. Whether a crowd of doubtful forms gets labelled species, sub-species or varieties makes no difference to us; nor does it matter, once well-marked varieties are admitted to exist, what rank the two or three hundred doubtful forms of British plants deserve. Yet individual variability on its own, together with a handful of well-marked varieties, does little to explain how species come into being in nature, however necessary it is as a foundation for the work. By what process have all those exquisite adaptations been perfected, adaptations of one part of the organisation to another, of the whole to the conditions of life, and of one distinct organic being to another? The woodpecker and the missletoe show these fine co-adaptations most clearly. They show scarcely less clearly in the lowliest parasite gripping the hairs of a quadruped or the feathers of a bird, in the build of the beetle that dives through water, and in the plumed seed carried off by the faintest breeze. Put briefly, fine adaptations appear everywhere, in every part of the organic world.

A further question arises: by what process do varieties, which I have termed incipient species, end up transformed into good and distinct species, forms that in most instances plainly differ from one another much more widely than varieties of a single species do? And how do those clusters of species come into being that make up what are known as distinct genera, and that stand further apart from one another than the species within one genus? Every one of these outcomes, as the next chapter will show in fuller detail, is an unavoidable consequence of the struggle for life. Because of this struggle for life, a variation of any kind, no matter how small or what produced it, will tend to preserve the individual of whatever species that carries it, provided it brings that individual some measure of advantage within its infinitely complex relations to other organic beings and to external nature; and the variation will, as a rule, pass to the individual's offspring. Those offspring in turn stand a better chance of living on, since only a small fraction of the many individuals of any species born in each generation can survive. To this principle, under which every slight variation is kept if it proves useful, I have given the name Natural Selection, so as to point out its kinship with man's power of selection. We have already seen that man, by selecting, can undoubtedly achieve great results and fit organic beings to his own purposes by piling up slight yet useful variations that the hand of Nature supplies to him. Natural Selection, however, as will appear later, is a power that never ceases to stand ready to act, and it surpasses man's feeble efforts by as immeasurable a margin as the works of Nature surpass those of Art.

Let us now examine the struggle for existence somewhat more closely. In a future work of mine the topic will receive the far fuller treatment it deserves. The elder De Candolle and Lyell have demonstrated at length, and in a philosophical spirit, that every organic being faces severe competition. Where plants are concerned, nobody has handled the matter with more spirit and skill than W. Herbert, Dean of Manchester, plainly owing to his great horticultural knowledge. Nothing comes more easily than granting in words that the struggle for life is universal, and nothing comes harder (so at least I have found) than keeping that conclusion steadily before the mind. And yet I am convinced that unless it is thoroughly engrained there, the whole economy of nature, together with every fact concerning distribution, rarity, abundance, extinction, and variation, will be perceived only dimly or misunderstood altogether. We look on the face of nature and find it bright with gladness; we frequently observe a superabundance of food. What we fail to see, or forget, is that the birds singing idly around us feed for the most part on insects or seeds and so are forever destroying life; we forget, too, how heavily those songsters, their eggs, and their nestlings are preyed upon by birds and beasts of prey; and we do not always keep in mind that food, superabundant though it may be at present, is not so in every season of each recurring year.

Let me be clear at the outset about the sense in which I use the phrase Struggle for Existence: I mean it broadly and metaphorically, so that it covers the dependence of one being upon another and, more importantly, not merely the survival of the individual but its success in leaving progeny. When two canine animals compete during a time of dearth, one can truly say they struggle with each other over which will get food and live. A plant at the edge of a desert, on the other hand, is commonly said to struggle for life against the drought, though it would be more accurate to say that it depends on the moisture. Consider a plant that annually produces a thousand seeds, of which on average only one comes to maturity: it can more truly be said to struggle with the plants of its own and other kinds that already clothe the ground. The missletoe depends on the apple and a few other trees, yet only in a far-fetched sense could it be said to struggle with those trees, since if too many of these parasites grow on one tree, that tree will languish and die. Several seedling missletoes growing close together on the same branch, however, may more truly be said to struggle with one another. Because the missletoe is disseminated by birds, its existence depends on birds, and it may metaphorically be said to struggle with other fruit-bearing plants in tempting birds to devour and thereby disseminate its seeds rather than those of other plants. These several senses shade into one another, and for convenience sake I gather them under the general term struggle for existence.

A struggle for existence follows inevitably from the high rate at which all organic beings tend to increase. Any being that produces several eggs or seeds over its natural lifetime must be destroyed at some period of its life, in some season or occasional year; otherwise, on the principle of geometrical increase, its numbers would soon grow so inordinately great that no country could support the product. Since more individuals are produced than can possibly survive, there must in every case be a struggle for existence, whether between one individual and another of the same species, between individuals of distinct species, or against the physical conditions of life. This is the doctrine of Malthus applied with manifold force to the whole animal and vegetable kingdoms, for here there can be no artificial increase of food and no prudential restraint from marriage. Some species may at present be increasing in numbers, more or less rapidly, but all cannot do so, because the world would not hold them.

Every organic being, without exception, tends to multiply at a rate so high that, were nothing to destroy it, the descendants of one pair would soon cover the earth. Man breeds slowly, yet he has doubled in twenty-five years, and at that pace there would literally be no standing room left for his progeny in less than a thousand years. Linnæus worked out that an annual plant yielding only two seeds, and no plant is as unproductive as that, whose seedlings each yielded two the following year, and so on, would give a million plants in twenty years. The elephant is held to be the slowest breeder among all known animals, and I have gone to some trouble to estimate its probable minimum rate of natural increase: it is safely below the mark to assume that it begins breeding at thirty years old, continues until ninety, produces six young over that interval, and survives to one hundred; on those assumptions, after 740 to 750 years there would be nearly nineteen million elephants alive, all descended from the first pair.

Yet on this question we possess evidence better than bare theoretical calculation, in the many recorded instances of animals in a state of nature multiplying at an astonishing pace when circumstances favoured them for two or three seasons running. More striking still is what our domestic animals of many kinds show, having gone wild in several parts of the world: had the reports of how fast slow-breeding cattle and horses multiplied in South America, and more recently in Australia, not been well authenticated, no one could have believed them. Plants tell the same story: instances could be cited of introduced plants that became common across entire islands within less than ten years. Several of the plants that now dominate the wide plains of La Plata, covering square leagues of ground almost to the exclusion of every other plant, came from Europe; and Dr. Falconer tells me of plants now ranging in India from Cape Comorin to the Himalaya that were brought in from America after its discovery. In cases like these, and endless others could be listed, nobody imagines that the fertility of the animals or plants concerned rose suddenly and temporarily by any sensible degree. The plain explanation is that the conditions of life were very favourable, so that fewer of the old and the young were destroyed and nearly all the young were able to breed. In such cases the geometrical ratio of increase, whose result never fails to surprise, is by itself enough to account for the extraordinarily rapid increase and wide diffusion of naturalised productions in their new homes.

In the wild, nearly every plant sets seed, and very few animals fail to pair each year. From this we can state with confidence that every plant and animal tends to multiply at a geometrical ratio, that each would very quickly fill any station in which it could exist at all, and that this geometrical tendency to increase must be held back by destruction at some stage of life. I think our acquaintance with the larger domestic animals leads us astray: we observe no great destruction falling on them, and we forget that thousands are killed for food every year, and that in a state of nature the same number would have to be got rid of in some way.
